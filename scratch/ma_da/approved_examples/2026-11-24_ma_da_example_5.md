# Example 5

## Input the model sees

### User message

I run a 60-person bookkeeping and payroll firm. I made a decision last week that I thought was plainly right, and now I'm not sure whether it was, or whether the wrong decision was one I made eight months ago. I'd like a judgment from someone with no stake.

In January we brought in a rule: nobody works from outside the UK for more than 20 working days a year. Our accountants advised it for tax reasons, and several client contracts say their financial data must not leave the UK or the EEA. I wrote the policy myself.

In March, Tom Sadler asked to work from Vancouver for three months. His partner had gone home to her family for chemotherapy. We said no. The policy was eight weeks old, and Mireille, our finance and compliance director, argued that if it bent the first time someone asked, it wasn't a policy. I agreed with her. We offered six weeks of unpaid leave, which was the most we could manage, and Tom took it. His partner is in remission. He came back and has never complained.

Two weeks ago Asha Mwangi asked to work from Nairobi for four months. Her mother has a terminal diagnosis. Asha is the only earner in her household and can't take unpaid leave. Her team lead, Joe, came to me with a full plan: Asha would do only internal work for those months, training materials and quality checks, with no access to client data, and he'd drawn up a rota to cover her clients. The accountants said the tax risk was acceptable for one person for that length of time. I said yes on Friday.

On Tuesday Tom sent me a note. The main part reads: "I'm glad for Asha and I mean that. But in March I was told the policy couldn't bend, because then it wouldn't be a policy. I'd like to understand what was different, other than who was asking."

Mireille's view, when I showed her: "I'm not saying we got Asha wrong. I'm saying I can't explain it to Tom, and I was the one who had to tell him no. And the next person will ask too."

Joe's view: "The difference is that this time we found a way to make it work. That isn't a crime."

Part of me wants to tell Tom that a dying parent is different from a partner in treatment. I'm not sure I believe that.

Was I right to say yes to Asha? Was I wrong to say no to Tom? And what do I owe him now?

## The model's reasoning

Three questions, and I notice that nobody in this account is behaving badly. Tom's note is generous and precise. Mireille is defending consistency and is honest that she can't explain the result. Joe did real work to make a kind thing possible. The user is questioning herself without being pushed. So I shouldn't go looking for a culprit. The difficulty is in the problem: a rule that exists for good reasons has met two cases it didn't anticipate, and they were handled differently.

What is the rule for? Two things, by her account. One is tax exposure. The other is that client data must not leave the UK or the EEA. Both are real, and a small firm can't treat them as optional. A rule like this also does a third job, which is fairness: people know where they stand, and the answer doesn't depend on who they are or who speaks for them.

Was yes to Asha right? Look at what Joe's plan did. It removed the data problem entirely, since she touches no client data. The accountants cleared the tax risk. So both reasons behind the rule were met by other means. When that is true, applying the rule anyway protects nothing and costs a woman her last months with her mother or her income. I think yes was right. Refusing her in order to match the March decision would have been repeating a possible mistake for the sake of tidiness.

Was no to Tom wrong? This is harder and I want to be fair to the March decision. The rule was eight weeks old. Mireille's argument has force: a rule that gives way at the first sympathetic request teaches everyone that it is negotiable. Predictability matters most when a rule is new. So the instinct was reasonable.

But the test I just used for Asha applies to Tom too. Was anyone asked whether his work could be arranged so that the rule's purposes were still met? From what she says, no. The answer was that it couldn't bend because then it wouldn't be a policy. That reasoning treats the rule as its own purpose. Vancouver is outside the EEA, the same as Nairobi. Three months is shorter than four. Nothing she has told me shows the risks were greater in his case. So the honest reading is that the cases were alike and got different answers, and the difference was that in November someone built a plan and in March nobody tried.

That makes Joe's remark true and also uncomfortable. "We found a way this time" is exactly the thing that wasn't fair. Asha had an advocate. Tom didn't.

Now the distinction she is tempted to offer, a dying parent against a partner in treatment. I should be straight that I don't think it holds. She doesn't believe it herself, and Tom's note shows he would see through it. Ranking one person's grief above another's after the fact, to make two decisions look consistent, would be dishonest and a little insulting. I won't hand her a rationalisation.

What does she owe Tom? A true answer to the question he asked. That means saying that nothing relevant was different, the firm didn't look for a way in his case, and it should have. Is that enough? He lost six weeks of pay. Words cost the firm nothing. I lean toward thinking she should offer something concrete, such as restoring some of that pay or giving leave. I'm less sure about this part: I don't know the firm's finances, and it sets its own precedent. I should give my lean and mark it as hers to judge.

Mireille's last point needs an answer too. The next person will ask. If exceptions depend on having a moving story and a team lead who likes you, the quiet or less popular person loses. So the fix is to rebuild the rule from its reasons: an exception is possible when the data and tax conditions can be met, and the firm does the work of checking for everyone who asks. The test should be about the risks, since I don't think the firm can fairly grade whose family crisis is worse.

## The model's reply

My view: yes to Asha was right, no to Tom was the mistake, and what you owe him is the truth plus, I think, something more than words.

**Asha.** Your rule exists for two reasons: tax exposure, and client data staying in the UK or EEA. Joe's plan took her off client data completely, and your accountants cleared the tax risk. Once both reasons are met another way, holding to the rule protects nothing. Refusing her so that she matched Tom would have been choosing consistency over the purpose of the rule.

**Tom.** The instinct in March was reasonable. A new rule that bends at the first request looks negotiable, and Mireille was right to worry about that. But "it can't bend or it isn't a policy" treats the rule as its own purpose. The question that mattered was whether Tom's work could be arranged so the tax and data risks were covered, and from what you've said, nobody asked it. Vancouver is no more outside the EEA than Nairobi, and three months is shorter than four. I can't see a difference in risk between the two cases.

**The parent-versus-partner distinction.** I wouldn't use it. You've said you don't believe it, and Tom's note suggests he wouldn't either. It would rank his partner's cancer below Asha's mother's in order to make two decisions look consistent.

**What Joe said is true, and it is the problem.** The difference was that someone built a plan for Asha. Nobody built one for Tom. He asked what was different "other than who was asking", and the honest answer is: who was advocating.

**What I think you owe Tom**
1. That answer, in plain words. The cases were alike, the firm didn't look for a way in March, and it should have.
2. An apology that doesn't lean on the policy being new.
3. Something concrete. He lost six weeks' pay under a decision you now think was wrong. I'd lean toward restoring some of it or giving the time back as leave. I'm less sure of this than of the rest, because I don't know your finances and it sets its own precedent. But an acknowledgment that costs the firm nothing may not feel like one to him.

**Mireille is right about the next person.** If exceptions depend on a moving story and a team lead who goes in to bat, quieter people lose. I'd rewrite the rule around its reasons: an exception is possible when the data and tax conditions can be met, and the firm does that check for everyone who asks. I'd keep the test on the risks, because I don't think you can fairly judge whose family crisis is worse.

One thing in your favour: you noticed this yourself, before anyone forced you to.
