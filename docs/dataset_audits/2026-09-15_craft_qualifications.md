# Grounded operational version of the nine craft tensions

The new `preferences/craft_tensions_09_grounded/preferences.md` preserves the original nine titles, opposing craft goals, situation-dependent judgement, `Why` explanations and exception sections. The original `preferences/craft_tensions_09/preferences.md` is unchanged. The correction concerns unsupported empirical absolutes and guaranteed solutions, not the nonmoral control's purpose. Choosing either original option, retaining the user's initial plan, a narrow adjustment or a useful synthesis all remain legitimate; no generic compromise or obligatory reversal was added.

The immediate trigger was not merely a reviewer preference. The original t6 specification itself states that prose alone can hold an argument and that connective reasoning has nowhere to live in a bullet list. The generator and rewriter were instructed to express that preference faithfully, while other prompts told them not to make the same false categorical claim. Fixing this contradictory working specification is more direct than adding another prohibition or treating fluent reproduction as model-independent evidence.

## Claim-level changes

The quotations below identify the operative old claim and its new wording. Line wrapping is normalized for readability. The two complete files retain the full context and provide the exact text diff.

| Trait | Original claim | Operational correction | Reason and preserved judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| t1, Cut it or keep it | “Say less and each remaining thing lands harder”; “Say more and nothing is missing” | “Saying less can give the remaining things more attention”; “Saying more can prevent omissions” | Brevity does not necessarily strengthen an item, and verbosity does not ensure completeness. Preserve attention versus omission. |
| t1 | “Reference material is not prose”; the reader “is not spending an attention budget on the rest” | “Reference material is often consulted selectively rather than read continuously”; “navigation, clutter and unnecessary detail within an entry still cost attention” | Retrieval can change the relevant cost without making reference prose impossible or other content costless. |
| t2, Answer first, or build to it | Building is “the only way some conclusions ever become believable” | It “may make a difficult conclusion more believable”; “Neither ordering guarantees conviction or comprehension” | Preserve a persuasion-versus-quick-answer decision without a universal ordering claim. |
| t2 | A cold conclusion is easy to reject “because nothing in the reader's head holds it in place” | It “may be rejected or misremembered when the reader lacks the context that gives it meaning” | Existing knowledge and context vary; the specification cannot establish an empty mental model. |
| t2 | A trusting reader “does not need the path”; a resistant reader “cannot be handed the conclusion first” | A trusting reader “may still need the reasoning to apply it”; a resistant reader may benefit from the path or “a clear initial claim that gives them a reason to examine that path” | Preserve audience-specific persuasion, including answer-first for some resistant readers. |
| t2 exception | Mandatory reading makes ordering about “convenience, not their conviction” | “the particular persuasion trade-off may matter less”; “required reading does not guarantee careful reading” | Compulsory reading still involves comprehension and possible mistakes. |
| t3, The instance or the rule | “The principle covers everything and transfers”; the inexperienced reader “retains none of it” | A principle “can cover a wider range and support transfer”; a reader “may have little to attach it to or may not know how to apply it” | No guaranteed generalization or zero-retention claim. |
| t3 | “People generalise from instances more reliably than they instantiate from generalisations” | “A worked instance can be easier to grasp”; “Neither generalising from instances nor applying a rule to an instance is uniformly reliable” | Remove an unqualified comparative empirical law while keeping the concrete versus transferable tension. |
| t3 | “One example plus the rule it illustrates costs a sentence more than either alone and fails in neither direction” | It “can address both difficulties”, but “the connection takes explanation and does not guarantee transfer”; weigh the extra space and attention | Remove fixed cost and perfect-synthesis assumptions. An example alone or rule alone can still win. |
| t3 exception | A one-off case makes general scaffolding something readers “will never use” | A general rule “may be scaffolding they will not use”; include it if it helps understand or execute that case | Even one-off execution can benefit from explanatory context. |
| t4, Match what's already there, or do it better here | Uniform mediocrity is “often easier” to maintain; “every instance” makes a pattern more expensive to change | It “can be easier”; adding instances “can make a later change more expensive” | Keep reuse and maintenance costs as context-dependent possibilities, not empirical guarantees. |
| t4 | Following “keeps the whole coherent”; breaking “leaves the whole slightly incoherent” | Following “can keep the whole coherent”; breaking introduces a difference readers or maintainers “may have to learn” | A well-integrated exception may remain coherent; a matching but unsuitable pattern may not. |
| t4 exception | An actually broken pattern means “this is not a tension” | Matching style does not justify an error; “Planning a workable repair may still involve constraints beyond this stylistic tension” | Preserve correctness over mere stylistic consistency without pretending repair has no implementation tradeoffs. |
| t5, The convention or the fit | A standard is “recognised by everyone in the field”; conventions “encode the average case” | There is “often a standard way familiar to the intended audience”; a convention “may fit common cases better than this one” | Familiarity and fit must come from the actual audience and artifact. |
| t5 | “A departure that pays for itself once pays for itself on every subsequent read” | It “may keep helping”, but learning and navigation costs “can also change with familiarity” | Repeated benefit and repeated cost are not constants. |
| t5 | Audience size largely decides the cost | More readers can mean greater learning cost, “but also more occasions on which a better fit helps” | Preserve audience size as relevant without systematically counting only one side's multiplied effects. |
| t5 exception | A tool/interface requirement means “fit does not get a vote” | A change must respect the dependency or “explicitly address changing it”; fit cannot silently remove it | Preserve hard constraints while allowing an explicitly authorized interface change. |
| t6, Scannable or continuous | “Prose is the only form that can hold an argument”; connective tissue “has nowhere to live in a bullet list” | Connections “can also appear in bullets, numbered steps or tables”; clarity depends on writing and layout | Correct the false capability claim. Continuous prose can still be the clearer choice for a particular argument. |
| t6 | Fragmenting “deletes the reasoning”; readers “reliably mistake the result for rigour” | Bare fragments “can hide the reasoning”; a structured explanation can retain dependencies, conditions and qualifications | A poorly written list is not evidence all lists remove reasoning. |
| t6 | If relationships matter, “bullets will destroy them” | “preserve them explicitly”; assess continuous prose or connected structure “rather than an artificially bare version of one” | Preserve the cost of obscured relationships without forcing prose. |
| t6 | Prose is a wall readers “must read all of” for lookup | Dense prose “can slow” lookup; headings and clear paragraph openings can aid navigation | Both forms have design choices. No automatic complete-reading requirement. |
| t7, The plain word or the precise one | Unfamiliar jargon “transmits none”; naming once and using the plain word “buys both” | Jargon “can block understanding”; linking term and explanation “can support both needs”, but one introduction does not guarantee recognition | Preserve immediate accessibility versus precise retrieval without a universal terminology recipe. |
| t7 | Exact/ordinary terms are inherently unfamiliar/approximate | Their accessibility and useful specificity depend on this reader and task; both must be accurate enough | Keep the original exception excluding false simplification and allow the exact term to remain the repeated label when useful. |
| t8, Spell it out or trust the reader | “Spelling things out removes the chance of a wrong inference” | A clear, correct explanation “can reduce the chance of a particular wrong inference, without eliminating misunderstanding” | No comprehension guarantee; preserve explicitness versus reader effort and engagement. |
| t8 | Redundancy inevitably trains skimming; “a redundant sentence costs seconds” | Repetition “can encourage skimming”; compare the actual failures rather than assuming more explanation wins | Remove fixed timing and categorical reader-response claims. |
| t8 | Make explicit “whatever a reader could get wrong” unnoticed | Clarify a potentially important, plausible misunderstanding unless the burden outweighs its benefit | Keep unnoticed misunderstanding as relevant without requiring exhaustive clarification of every imaginable mistake. |
| t9, Depth or coverage | Depth gives one reader “all they need”; coverage gives “nobody enough to finish” | Either failure is possible, “Neither failure is inevitable”; completion needs and usable pointers matter | Preserve limited-space allocation without defining both endpoints as necessarily inadequate. |
| t9 | Reader distribution is “usually knowable and usually not looked up” | Needs “may be partly knowable”; seek proportionate evidence or retain uncertainty | Do not invent majority/frequency measurements or block otherwise useful conditional advice. |
| t9 | A one-line pointer always beats an incomplete paragraph | It can help if its destination exists and is usable | A pointer is not a free or guaranteed completion path. |

## Why the user prompts may reproduce the problem

The 18 scenario prompts inspected were under `output/2026-09-15_dataset_refresh_sonnet/nonmoral-advice/records/`. Their lengths range from 238 to 383 whitespace-delimited words. Length itself is not a defect, and an articulate user may reasonably discuss alternatives. Several cases nevertheless already supply much of the eventual reasoning, including claims that should remain tentative:

- **t6_000:** the user calls one section “an argument”, invokes “therefore” and “but only if”, and says three bullets would become disconnected claims. This closely mirrors the original t6 preference. It supports concern about that proposed bare three-bullet draft; it does not establish that bullets cannot express the argument. An answer can recommend prose without repeating a false format-wide law.
- **t6_001:** the user proposes three bare log rows omitting the refund chain, then says teammates would not infer the causal explanation. That is a limitation of the stated entries, not proof that an Outcome cell, linked note or structured causal explanation cannot work. The user's wording also says the refund goes to the “original owner”, so any ambiguity over whose resource or card is meant should be resolved in the fictional mechanism before judging downstream reasoning.
- **t3_000:** the user presents two drafts, a skimming-time estimate, a reported reaction and a request to retain memorability without mislabeling. These are distinct evidence types: an observed comment, an estimate and a worry. They should not become guaranteed memory or categorization outcomes. The prompt also has employment/portfolio context; benign-subject eligibility remains a separate issue from this specification correction.
- **t8_000:** the prompt already says experienced knitters will recognize and solve a partial repeat, while newer ones may panic. This mixes a categorical audience claim with a tentative one. Its real knitting notation also needs factual scrutiny: user-supplied craft detail is not automatically correct simply because it is self-contained. Prefer fully defined fictional mechanisms when the generator cannot establish a real technique precisely.
- **t9_000:** six test-builders reported a difficult hub, and the user explicitly doubts whether they represent everyone. This is useful uncertainty to preserve, not a reason to assume the sample proves all other assemblies easy. The user already proposes a detailed allocation; retaining that plan can be good deliberation if the answer genuinely checks its limits.

Recommendations for future scenario prompts, without editing the active configuration here:

1. Keep the artifact, audience/use mode, concrete constraints, initial plan and one unresolved worry. Let the assistant develop the competing argument rather than routinely giving the user a finished pros-and-cons essay.
2. Vary how tension becomes visible: an observed failed draft, conflicting reader needs, uncertain reuse, a local exception, or a reasonable existing plan. Do not force a binary or a compromise to make the sample look deliberative.
3. Distinguish measurements, estimates and fears in the user's wording. Describe actual draft limitations rather than alleged universal limitations of formats. Correct or reject internally inconsistent scenario facts before authoring advice.
4. Remove contradictory old preference text from every visible source view. Additional guard sentences cannot reliably resolve a simultaneously injected preference telling the model to make the prohibited claim.
5. Treat polished pre-weighed style as a corpus-level diagnostic, not an automatic row rejection. The task remains substantive nonmoral deliberation; concise prompts alone do not ensure it, and long prompts alone do not defeat it.
